Who this is for

You know some JavaScript and some linear algebra — a matrix multiply and a dot product do not scare you — and you would like to build a label-occlusion system, or understand the one in iTowns well enough to change it. No graphics, cartography or algorithms background is assumed. Everything is derived: when a formula appears, it is worked through on real numbers first.

The measurements come from the benchmark built for this work (poster Occlusion d'étiquettes cartographiques dans un environnement 3D, Ciril GROUP R&D / CESI, August 2026). Two live benches ship with the repo — examples/collision_configurator.html sweeps every combination described here, and examples/collision_bounds_bench.html isolates the collider models.

The three sentences that the rest of the course elaborates

  1. The collider decides quality. How tightly you approximate a label's shape is what governs how many labels fit. Changing the collider moves quality by 15 points; changing the data structure moves it by zero.
  2. The ordering decides cost. Sorting several thousand candidates every frame is a bigger line item than the collision tests themselves, which is why a sort that does no comparisons at all is worth the trouble.
  3. The occupancy structure is a speed choice, not a quality choice — with exactly one exception, the raster bitmap, which changes the geometry of the question and therefore does move quality (and stability, in the good direction).
Part I

The problem

What is being decided, why it cannot be decided exactly, and how we will know whether an answer is any good.

1

Labels, and why 3D is different

A label is a piece of text that names something on a map. Deciding which labels to draw is an editorial job on a paper map and an every-frame computation in a 3D one.

Map labels come in two geometric flavours, and the difference runs through this entire course:

  • Point labels name a place that is a position: a city, a monument, a shop. They are drawn horizontally, because every renderer keeps them horizontal — MapLibre resolves text-rotation-alignment to viewport for point placement, so a city name never tilts, no matter how the camera turns.
  • Line labels name a place that is a path: a street, a river, a rail line. They follow the geometry of that path, which means they are rotated and usually curved.

On a printed map, a cartographer chooses in advance which names appear. That choice is expensive, careful, and permanent. In a 3D viewer the camera moves continuously: the set of visible features, their sizes on screen and their overlaps all change every frame, so the selection has to be recomputed from nothing, sixty times a second.

The scale is the part that surprises people. Under the name of a single city, a dataset holds thousands of streets, and each street serves hundreds of addressable places. On a view of Paris with an IGN dataset, the number of labels resident in memory reaches 24 000 during a particularly dense frame. Drawing them all produces an unreadable grey mat of text. Drawing a fixed subset produces a map that is empty when you zoom in and cluttered when you zoom out.

So three requirements pull against each other, and no algorithm satisfies all three:

Density

Show as much information as possible. Every label you drop is a name the user cannot read.

Legibility

No two drawn labels may overlap. One overlap destroys two names, so an overlap costs double.

Stability

A label must not flicker on and off between consecutive frames. This one is easy to forget when you benchmark on still frames, and it is the requirement users notice first. The poster's French term is basculement — a "switch" — and this course calls the metric churn.

Density and legibility are in direct opposition: the only way to guarantee no overlaps is to draw fewer labels. Stability opposes both: the most legible, densest arrangement for this frame is usually not the arrangement for the next one, and swapping to it is exactly the flicker you must avoid.

Scope

This course covers collision detection and selection: given a set of labels with fixed anchor positions, find the overlaps and choose which labels survive. It does not cover placement — moving a label to a better spot near its anchor (above the point, below it, to the left…), which is a related and much larger literature. The bench includes a positional-candidate mode (PAL, K positions per label) so the two can be compared, but everything measured here uses one fixed position per label.

2

The problem has a name, and it is NP-hard

Written down formally, label selection is Maximum Weight Independent Set. That is bad news with a useful silver lining.

Build a graph. Every candidate label is a vertex, with a weight equal to how much you care about showing it (a capital city outranks a footpath). Draw an edge between two vertices whenever their footprints overlap on screen — those two labels cannot both be drawn.

An independent set in a graph is a set of vertices with no edge between any two of them: exactly a set of labels that can all be drawn together. You want the independent set whose total weight is largest. That is MWIS, and it is NP-hard — no known algorithm finds the exact answer in time that stays reasonable as the graph grows. With 6 000 candidates you are not going to solve it in 16 milliseconds. You are not going to solve it in an hour.

Two things rescue the situation.

First, the graph is not arbitrary. Its edges come from geometry: labels only conflict with labels that are near them on screen. A label of size w×h can only touch others within that distance, so vertex degree is bounded by how many labels fit in a neighbourhood, not by n. That is why every structure in Part V works: you never need to compare a label against all the others, only against the handful that could possibly be nearby.

Second, we do not need the optimum — we need to know how far from it we are. This is the single most important methodological decision in the whole project. Once, offline, with no time limit, you compute a near-optimal arrangement for a fixed scene using simulated annealing. That arrangement is your 100 %. Every real-time algorithm is then scored as a percentage of it. In the poster's bench, that offline solve takes 22 069 ms for a scene the real-time pipeline handles in 0.983 ms — roughly 22 000× the budget, which is exactly why it can only ever be a yardstick.

Result

Greedy-by-priority, with exact geometry and no tolerance, reaches 77.7 % of that annealed ceiling. The shipped configuration, which trades some of that away for speed and stability, reaches 68.4 %. Neither number is close to 100, and that is not a defect: the ceiling is an offline solver given unlimited time on a frozen frame.

3

The pipeline

Three stages, run once per frame. Everything else in this course is a choice inside one of them.

The shipped implementation is packages/Label/src/Collision/CollisionCore.ts, and it is worth reading now, because it is only about seventy lines of real work. Its three phases are literally labelled:

// Phase 1: candidate filter + score
for (const label of labels) {
    label.isCandidate = label.visible && label.opacity > 0 && label.glyphs.length > 0
                     && label.bounds.width > 0 && projector.checkVisible(label);
    if (!label.isCandidate) { label.shouldRender = false; continue; }
    const distSq = dx*dx + dy*dy + dz*dz;                // camera → label
    label.score = distSq * ((baseFontSize / label.fontSize) ** fontSizePriorityPower);
    cands.push(label);
}

// Phase 2: priority ordering
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    let ls = Math.log1p(cands[i].score);
    if (!cands[i].shouldRender) { ls *= renderPenaltyMultiplier; }   // hysteresis
    keys[i] = ls;
}
const m = ordering.run(cands, keys, n, logMin, logMax, ordered);

// Phase 3: occlusion placement
for (let s = 0; s < m; s++) {
    const label = ordered[s], was = label.shouldRender;
    if (!projector.project(label, aabb)) { label.shouldRender = false; continue; }
    const sr = occ.testRect(aabb.x0, aabb.y0, aabb.x1, aabb.y1, was);
    if (sr) { occ.markRect(aabb.x0, aabb.y0, aabb.x1, aabb.y1); }
    label.shouldRender = sr;
}

packages/Label/src/Collision/CollisionCore.ts — condensed

Notice what is not there. No backtracking. No second attempt at a different position. No global reasoning of any kind. A label gets one look, in one order, and the answer is final for that frame. Every improvement in this course happens inside one of those three phases, and the interfaces between them never change:

  • Phase 1 hands phase 2 a float key per candidate. Any ordering algorithm can consume that.
  • Phase 2 hands phase 3 an array of labels in attempt order. Phase 3 does not care how it was sorted.
  • Phase 3 talks to the occupancy structure through four methods: testRect, markRect, testQuad, markQuad. Any structure implementing those is a drop-in.
Why this decomposition matters

Because the three axes are independent, the benchmark can sweep them as a product — ordering × occupancy × collider × parameters — and attribute a result to the axis that caused it. Most published comparisons of labelling algorithms conflate the three, which is how you get folklore like "R-trees give better labelling than grids". They cannot: chapter 19 shows they compute the identical predicate.

4

How quality is measured

Three numbers per configuration: how much text you showed, how long it took, and how much it flickered. Getting these definitions right matters more than any optimisation that follows.

The scene

Measurements need a scene that behaves like a map, because a scene of uniformly scattered labels understates the problem. Real labels are clustered: a dense town centre, then empty fields. Clustering is what creates both the crowded regions where the algorithm has to make hard choices and the empty regions where it has nothing to do. The bench generates four classes in the proportions a map actually has — a few large city names, more towns, many roads, a great many small POIs — using a Thomas cluster process, with roads laid along corridors between settlement centres.

Label weight (its importance) is per class, and a near label counts for more than a far one — a metric that ignored distance would happily throw away the label under the cursor to keep one on the horizon.

Quality: percentage of an offline optimum

Quality is the share of label weight actually displayed, divided by the best arrangement achievable on the same scene. The denominator is computed once, offline, by simulated annealing: start from the greedy arrangement, then repeatedly propose swaps (drop a drawn label, add one or more that it was blocking), accepting improvements always and accepting small regressions with a probability that decays — which is what lets it climb out of local optima that greedy cannot escape. The bench reports what fraction of the ceiling was proven exactly versus annealed, so you know how much to trust it.

Two properties make this a good metric and one makes it dangerous:

  • It is comparable across scenes: 68 % means the same thing at 1 500 labels and at 24 000, whereas "1 200 labels drawn" does not.
  • It is bounded: no configuration can exceed 100 %, so an implausible score is a bug signal.
  • But it is weight-weighted, so a configuration can raise its score by showing a few heavy city names and dropping many POIs. Always read the raw placed count next to it.

Churn: the stability metric

Churn (basculement) counts, per frame, the labels whose drawn/not-drawn state changed, as a share of the labels on screen. A label appearing is a switch; a label disappearing is a switch. Two details make the metric honest:

  • It is normalised by the labels on screen, so a configuration cannot win by drawing almost nothing.
  • Vanish and appear are reported separately as well as summed, because a label disappearing under a static camera is far more jarring than one arriving.

Time: the placement pass, not the frame

The reported millisecond figure is the cost of the collision pass itself — candidate filter, ordering, placement — averaged over the camera path, and measured under a load ramp of 1 500 / 6 000 / 24 000 labels at 1920 × 1080 over 60 frames. It excludes rendering, text layout and GPU upload, which are large and would drown out the differences being measured.

Read tables within themselves

Absolute milliseconds are run-relative: the machine, the browser's JIT state and the scene seed all move them. In the poster the chosen configuration appears at 1.127 ms in the ordering table and 0.983 ms in the comparison table — same configuration, different runs. Compare rows inside a table, never numbers across tables, and this course keeps each table's numbers together for that reason.

Part II

Getting to pixels

A collision test compares screen rectangles, so before any of it can happen a 3D position and a string of glyphs have to become a box in pixels. This is the part most write-ups skip, and it is where the bugs live.

5

From a world position to a screen box

Four matrix rows, one divide, one viewport map — done four times, once per corner.

A label is anchored at a point in world space, and its text is a flat rectangle whose size you want expressed in pixels, not in metres: a city name is 100 px wide on screen whether the camera is 200 m or 20 km away. So the transform is not simply "project a rectangle"; the rectangle's world size has to be derived from the distance first. Here is the whole chain.

Step 1 — world to camera space

Multiply the world position by the camera's inverse world matrix (three(.js) calls it matrixWorldInverse). Written out on the raw element array, so no object allocation happens in a hot loop:

const cvx = ve[0]*p.x + ve[4]*p.y + ve[8] *p.z + ve[12];
const cvy = ve[1]*p.x + ve[5]*p.y + ve[9] *p.z + ve[13];
const cvz = ve[2]*p.x + ve[6]*p.y + ve[10]*p.z + ve[14];
if (cvz >= 0) { return false; }   // behind the camera: three.js looks down −z

That cvz >= 0 test is the cheapest cull you have, and it must come first: everything downstream divides by a quantity that is meaningless behind the camera.

Step 2 — the four corners, at constant pixel size

The label's own box is bounds.width × bounds.height, expressed in font pixels at a reference font size. Convert to world units, then scale by distance:

worldScale = (label.fontSize / baseFontSize) / pxPerUnit;   // px → world units
viewDepth  = √(cvx² + cvy² + cvz²);                          // distance to camera
localX     = cornerX · worldScale · viewDepth;               // grows with distance…

…and then perspective division shrinks it back by the same factor, leaving a constant size in pixels. pxPerUnit is the conversion constant between font pixels and world units (1024 by default); baseFontSize is the size the glyph atlas was built at (24).

For a viewport-aligned label — every point label — the corner offsets are added directly in camera space, which is what keeps the text horizontal on screen no matter where the camera is:

vx = cvx + localX;   vy = cvy + localY;   vz = cvz;

For a world-aligned label — one that lies on the ground, or along a street — the offset is rotated by the label's quaternion in world space first, then transformed into camera space. That is the only difference between the two alignments, and it is why rotation is free for point labels: there is nothing to rotate.

Step 3 — projection, divide, viewport

const cx = pe[0]*vx + pe[4]*vy + pe[8] *vz + pe[12];
const cy = pe[1]*vx + pe[5]*vy + pe[9] *vz + pe[13];
const cw = pe[3]*vx + pe[7]*vy + pe[11]*vz + pe[15];
if (cw <= 0) { return false; }              // corner behind the eye
const ndcX = cx / cw, ndcY = cy / cw;              // −1 … +1
px = (ndcX *  0.5 + 0.5) * screenW;                   // 0 … W
py = (ndcY * -0.5 + 0.5) * screenH;                   // y flips: NDC is up, pixels are down

The sign flip on y is the classic source of "my labels collide correctly but upside down" bugs. NDC has +y upward; screen pixels have +y downward.

Step 4 — box or quad

With four projected corners in hand there are two ways to continue, and the choice is the collider question of Part III:

  • Axis-aligned box: take the min and max of the four x values and of the four y values, floor and ceil to whole pixels, clamp to the viewport. Four numbers.
  • Rotated quad: keep the four corners as they are. Eight numbers, and a much more expensive test.
A worked example

A 15-character POI label at fontSize 15, with baseFontSize 24 and pxPerUnit 1024. Its measured bounds are 92 × 11 font-px. Then worldScale = (15/24)/1024 = 6.1 × 10⁻⁴, so at a viewDepth of 3 000 world units the corner offsets in camera space are 92 × 6.1e-4 × 3000 ≈ 168 units across. That looks enormous — until the projection divides by w ≈ 3 000 and a 50° vertical field of view over 1080 px, leaving the label about 57 px wide on screen. Change the distance to 30 000 and both numbers scale by ten, so the pixel width does not move.

The pre-filter

Projecting four corners per label is not free, and most labels in a large dataset are off-screen. So the candidate phase runs a cheap conservative test first (checkVisible): project the anchor point only, then test it against the NDC cube expanded by a margin large enough to cover the label's own extent:

marginNDC = (max(bw, bh) · worldScale · pe[0]) / ccw + ndcCullMargin;
if (|ndcCx| > 1 + marginNDC || |ndcCy| > 1 + marginNDC) return false;

The margin is what makes this conservative rather than wrong: a label whose anchor sits just off screen may still have half its text on screen, and must survive the filter. Rejecting it early would make text pop in at the screen edge.

6

A label's own geometry

Where bounds.width comes from, why the height must be the ink and not the line box, and how anchors move the box around the point.

Advances, not character counts

The naive way to size a label is chars × 0.55 × fontSize. Do not: real fonts are proportional, an i is a third of an m, and per-glyph colliders (chapter 10) are meaningless without real per-glyph metrics. Sum the advances instead:

bw = 0; maxH = 0;
for (const g of glyphs) {
    bw += g.advance;                       // pen movement, includes side bearings
    maxH = Math.max(maxH, g.inkHeight);    // tallest inked extent
}
label.bounds = { width: bw, height: maxH };

An advance is how far the pen moves after drawing a glyph — slightly more than the glyph's ink, because it includes the side bearings that keep letters from touching. Summing advances therefore gives the true set width of the string.

The 81 % mistake

Using a CSS-style line height for the label's height — fontSize × 1.3 — reserves 1.81× the actual inked height of the text. That is 81 % dead space in every single point label, before any horizontal waste, and it silently drops labels that would have fit. MapLibre does not do this: its collision box comes straight from the shaping result's top and bottom, i.e. the glyph extent, with pixel padding added separately at collision time. Use the ink.

Anchors and offsets

The anchor decides where the box sits relative to the point. It is a pure offset applied to the local corners before projection:

A user-supplied offset in em units (× fontSize) is added on top, which is how you nudge a label above its marker. Both are camera-independent, so the corner array is computed once per label and cached, keyed by pxPerUnit so a resolution change invalidates it. In a frame with 6 000 candidates that cache is the difference between 6 000 and 24 000 multiplications — small, but this is the loop where small things are the whole cost.

Curved labels: a baseline, not a box

A line label does not sit in a rectangle at all; its glyphs are placed along a curve. The bench models that curve as a sine, deliberately, because a constant-curvature arc just leans the whole label one way while a real road S-bends — and an S-bend is what stresses every chain-of-primitives approximation:

lateral(u) = amp · sin(2π · waves · u + phase),   u ∈ [−0.5, +0.5]

Each glyph then needs a position and a tangent angle along that curve. There is one non-obvious requirement here, and getting it wrong produces a bug that looks like a rendering artefact:

Place glyphs by arc length, not by x

It is tempting to treat the cumulative advance as an x position: t = advance / totalWidth. That is only valid on a straight baseline. On a bend, equal steps in x do not cover equal distance along the curve: where the slope is steep, a fixed x-step covers more real arc length, so glyphs bunch up there and spread out on the flat stretches — visibly collapsing on themselves at high curvature. A multi-wave sine has no closed-form arc length, so build a small lookup table once (64 samples of cumulative chord length), then invert it: given "fraction of total arc length so far", interpolate the t that reaches it. Cost is one table per label at build time, zero per frame.

Part III

Collision math

Four ways to ask "do these two labels touch?", from four integer comparisons to a full separating-axis test — what each one costs in operations, and what each one buys in labels displayed.

7

AABB: the four comparisons

The axis-aligned bounding box is the cheapest useful collider in existence. Understand its test completely and every other test in this part is a variation on it.

One dimension first

Two intervals A = [a₀, a₁] and B = [b₀, b₁] on a line. They fail to overlap in exactly two ways: A ends before B starts (a₁ < b₀), or B ends before A starts (b₁ < a₀). Negate that and you have the overlap condition:

overlap₁D  ⇔  a₀ ≤ b₁  and  b₀ ≤ a₁

Two comparisons. No arithmetic at all.

Two dimensions: an AND of two 1-D tests

An axis-aligned box is a pair of intervals, one per axis — that is the whole point of the representation. Two boxes overlap only if they overlap on both axes, because a gap on either axis is a plane that separates them:

overlap  ⇔  a.x0 ≤ b.x1  and  b.x0 ≤ a.x1
         and  a.y0 ≤ b.y1  and  b.y0 ≤ a.y1
Two conventions to fix early

Inclusive or exclusive bounds. With integer pixel bounds and ≤, two boxes that share an edge column count as overlapping. That is usually what you want for text (adjacent glyphs from different labels look joined), but it must be consistent between the test and whatever ground truth you score against, or your quality numbers will drift by a percent for no visible reason.

Padding is part of the collider. Real renderers add a few pixels of padding so labels do not merely avoid overlapping but stay visually separate — MapLibre's text-padding. Add it once, when the box is built, not at test time.

What it gets wrong: the envelope of a rotated label

An AABB around a rotated label is its envelope, and the envelope of a long thin rotated rectangle is enormous. For a w × h rectangle rotated by θ:

W′ = w·|cos θ| + h·|sin θ|
H′ = w·|sin θ| + h·|cos θ|

Take a street label 200 × 20 px at 30°: W′ = 183, H′ = 117, so the envelope is 21 500 px² around 4 000 px² of text — 5.4× the area, and it peaks at 6.05× at 45°. Every one of those wasted pixels blocks another label. This single fact is why the collider question dominates quality, and it is why the answer differs for the two label kinds:

  • Point labels are never rotated (θ = 0), so their AABB is not an approximation at all — it is exact, up to the gaps between letters.
  • Line labels are always rotated and usually curved, so a single AABB is the worst possible choice for them. Measured, in the bench's controlled mode: whole-label AABB waste rises from 2.13× to 5.88× once labels rotate, while oriented models do not move at all.

That asymmetry is the shape of the final answer: plain boxes for point labels, subdivided colliders for line labels.

8

OBB and the separating-axis theorem

The exact test for two rotated rectangles. It is four projections and four comparisons, and once you have seen it drawn it stops being intimidating.

The representation

An oriented bounding box is a centre, two perpendicular unit axes and two half-extents:

C  = centre (cx, cy)
u  = unit vector along the text direction  = (cos θ, sin θ)
v  = perpendicular unit vector             = (−sin θ, cos θ)
e₁ = half width along u
e₂ = half height along v

Note that v is not independent — for a rectangle it is u turned 90°. So a 2-D OBB is five numbers, and a rotated label already gives you all five.

The theorem, in the only form you need

For two convex shapes, if there exists a line onto which their projections (their "shadows") do not overlap, the shapes do not intersect. Such a line is a separating axis. For convex polygons it is enough to test the axes perpendicular to each polygon's edges — and a rectangle only has two distinct edge directions. So two rectangles need four candidate axes: uA, vA, uB, vB.

Projecting a box onto a direction L gives an interval centred at C·L whose half-length is

r = e₁·|u·L| + e₂·|v·L|

which reads exactly as it looks: how far the box reaches along L is how far its own half-extents reach along L, added up with absolute values because a box reaches equally in both directions. Then the two projections are separated when the distance between their centres, measured along L, exceeds the sum of their reaches:

|(C_B − C_A) · L|  >  r_A + r_B      ⇒  separated  ⇒  no collision

The code, with the simplification that halves it

When L is A's own axis uA, then uA·uA = 1 and vA·uA = 0, so r_A collapses to e₁ᴬ — no dot products needed for the box that owns the axis:

function obbOverlap(A, B) {
    const dx = B.cx - A.cx, dy = B.cy - A.cy;
    // A's axes: u = (c, s), v = (-s, c)
    const ca = A.cos, sa = A.sin, cb = B.cos, sb = B.sin;
    // cross terms, computed once and reused on all four axes
    const uu = ca*cb + sa*sb;      // u_A · u_B  = cos(θB − θA)
    const uv = ca*(-sb) + sa*cb;   // u_A · v_B  = −sin(θB − θA)
    const au = Math.abs(uu), av = Math.abs(uv);

    // axis u_A : r_A = A.e1 ; r_B = B.e1·|u_B·u_A| + B.e2·|v_B·u_A|
    if (Math.abs(dx*ca + dy*sa) > A.e1 + B.e1*au + B.e2*av) return false;
    // axis v_A
    if (Math.abs(-dx*sa + dy*ca) > A.e2 + B.e1*av + B.e2*au) return false;
    // axis u_B
    if (Math.abs(dx*cb + dy*sb) > B.e1 + A.e1*au + A.e2*av) return false;
    // axis v_B
    if (Math.abs(-dx*sb + dy*cb) > B.e2 + A.e1*av + A.e2*au) return false;
    return true;   // no separating axis exists → they overlap
}

Count the work in the worst case: 4 multiplies for the cross terms, then per axis 2 multiplies for the centre projection and 2 for the other box's reach, plus adds, an absolute value and a comparison. That is about 20 multiplications, 16 additions and 4 branches, against the AABB's 4 comparisons and no multiplications. Roughly an order of magnitude, which matches what the bench measures end-to-end once you account for everything else in a frame.

The mistake that makes SAT look slow

Computing cos θ and sin θ inside the pair test. There are n labels and potentially many pairs per label; trig belongs in the per-label projection pass, cached on the label, so the pair test only ever sees numbers. A profile showing "SAT is dominated by Math.cos" is a profile of the wrong loop.

Testing a rotated quad against a raster instead

There is a second exact way to handle an oriented shape, and the shipped bitmap uses it: instead of comparing it against other shapes, rasterise it and compare it against occupied cells. Decompose the quad into horizontal spans — for each row of the raster, find where the quad's edges cross that row's top and bottom, and take the extreme x values:

for (y = ⌊minY⌋ … ⌈maxY⌉) {
    lo = +∞; hi = −∞;
    for (each edge (a → b)) {
        // where does this edge cross the row's top and bottom?
        if (edge straddles y)   { x = a.x + (b.x−a.x)·(y − a.y)/(b.y − a.y); lo = min(lo,x); hi = max(hi,x); }
        if (edge straddles y+1) { … same at y+1 … }
        if (a.y ∈ [y, y+1])     { lo = min(lo, a.x); hi = max(hi, a.x); }   // vertex inside the band
    }
    emit span (y, ⌊lo⌋, ⌈hi⌉);
}

The third clause is the one people forget, and the reason spans are taken over the whole band [y, y+1] rather than at a single scanline: without it, a thin diagonal sliver can pass between two sample rows and be treated as empty. Conservative spans are the correct choice here — a collider that occasionally claims one pixel too many loses a fraction of a percent of quality; one that misses a sliver draws two labels on top of each other, which is the failure the whole system exists to prevent.

9

Circle chains, and why MapLibre uses them

A circle has no orientation, so a chain of circles handles curvature without any rotation math at all.

Mapbox and MapLibre approximate a line label as a series of circles laid along its baseline, each roughly as large as the text is tall. The overlap test between two circles is the cheapest exact test in geometry — compare squared distance against squared radius sum, so no square root is ever needed:

dx = c₂.x − c₁.x;  dy = c₂.y − c₁.y;  r = c₁.r + c₂.r;
overlap  ⇔  dx·dx + dy·dy < r·r        // 2 subs, 3 mults, 1 add, 1 compare

And circle-versus-box, which is what you need when a curved line label meets a horizontal point label, is barely worse — clamp the circle centre into the box, then measure:

qx = clamp(c.x, box.x0, box.x1);   qy = clamp(c.y, box.y0, box.y1);
overlap  ⇔  (c.x−qx)² + (c.y−qy)² < c.r²

Where the cost goes

A chain has k primitives, so a chain-versus-chain comparison is k × m circle tests unless a broad phase intervenes — which is exactly why every real implementation puts the chain inside a grid and tests only the circles in shared cells. MapLibre keeps k small by construction: the circle count follows the label's length divided by the spacing, and long labels are simply not placed when they do not fit.

What MapLibre actually does

Reading collision_index.ts: point labels get one axis-aligned box — the envelope of the rotated quad, since rotation alone never produces circles — and only line-placed labels get a circle chain. So MapLibre is already a hybrid: box for points, chain for lines. That is the same shape of answer this course arrives at; the difference is only in what the line collider is made of, boxes rather than circles, which turns out to be both tighter and cheaper.

The rule you must not break

A multi-piece collider must test every piece before marking any piece. If you test-and-mark piece by piece, glyph 2 collides with the region glyph 1 just claimed, and the label rejects itself. This bug is easy to miss because the label still looks plausible — you just place far fewer labels than you should, uniformly, with no obvious artefact to point at. In the shipped core the two operations are separate methods (testRect then markRect) precisely so that the loop over pieces can be written correctly.

Adaptive grouping: how many pieces, and where to cut

Per-glyph is the tightest but costs n tests per label. Grouping consecutive glyphs is the middle ground, and the criterion for closing a group is the same one line-simplification algorithms use (Douglas–Peucker): keep adding glyphs while they stay close to a straight line, and cut when they do not.

for (g = 1; g <= chars; g++) {
    split = (g === chars) || (g − start) >= maxGlyphsPerGroup;
    if (!split) {
        // perpendicular distance of glyph g from the tangent at the group's start
        dev = |−(u_g − u_s)·sin θ_s + (v_g − v_s)·cos θ_s|;
        split = dev > segTolerancePx;
    }
    if (split) { emitGroup(start, g − start); start = g; }
}

Two knobs, and they do different jobs:

  • Deviation tolerance (px) subdivides curved text: a sharp bend closes a group early.
  • Maximum glyphs per group subdivides straight text, where the deviation is always zero and would otherwise produce one label-sized box. With a cap of 1 this degenerates to per-glyph.

Orienting a group box correctly

Once a group is chosen you need its box. Taking a plain min/max of the glyph corners gives an axis-aligned box (fine — that is the per-group AABB). For an oriented group box, the reference angle matters, and the obvious choice is wrong:

  • Do not use the tangent at the group's midpoint glyph. On a circular arc that works, because curvature is constant. On a real road — or the bench's multi-wave sine — curvature varies within a group, and the midpoint tangent then mis-describes how the glyphs are actually spread out, producing a visibly tilted, loose box.
  • Do use the chord direction: the vector from the first glyph's centre to the last. It reflects the group's real span regardless of how curvature varies inside it.

With the angle chosen, de-rotate every glyph corner into that frame, take min/max there, then rotate the four resulting corners back:

θ  = atan2(v_last − v_first, u_last − u_first);      // chord, not midpoint tangent
// for every corner of every glyph in the group:
lx =  wx·cos θ + wy·sin θ;   ly = −wx·sin θ + wy·cos θ;   // into the group frame
// min/max lx, ly  →  4 local corners  →  rotate back:
qx = lx·cos θ − ly·sin θ;    qy = lx·sin θ + ly·cos θ;

Part IV

Ordering

The greedy pass has no lookahead, so the order it walks the candidates in is the algorithm. This part builds a sort that produces exactly the order a comparison sort would, without ever comparing two labels — and explains, with the operation counts, where the time actually goes.

12

Priority is the algorithm

First come, first served. So whoever comes first had better deserve it.

In the placement loop, a label that is attempted earlier gets its space and keeps it. Nothing later can evict it. So the ordering carries the entire editorial policy of the map: which labels matter, in what circumstances. The shipped key has three ingredients.

Distance, squared

Nearer labels win. Squared distance is used rather than distance because the square root is pointless — it is a monotone function, so it cannot change any ordering, and skipping it saves 6 000 square roots per frame:

distSq = dx·dx + dy·dy + dz·dz;      // camera → label anchor

Size, as a tunable power

Bigger type means a more important feature, so a large label should beat a small one even from further away. The shipped form divides the reference font size by the label's own, raised to a configurable exponent:

score = distSq · (baseFontSize / fontSize) ^ fontSizePriorityPower;

A label twice the reference size gets its effective distance halved (at power 1), so it wins against smaller labels up to √2 times further away. Setting the power to 0 removes size from the policy entirely and orders purely by distance; raising it makes the map favour big names aggressively. It is the one genuinely editorial knob in the pipeline.

The compression, and why it exists

Squared distances span an enormous dynamic range — from a few units to 10¹⁰ across a globe view — and everything downstream wants a bounded, evenly-populated key domain. So the key is compressed logarithmically:

key = log1p(score);            // log(1 + score): defined and precise near 0

log1p rather than log because scores near zero (a label at the camera) would otherwise produce large negative numbers or a division by zero. The log is monotone, so again the ordering is untouched — but the distribution of keys becomes roughly uniform instead of extremely skewed, and that is what makes bucketing and quantisation behave (chapter 17).

The stability term

if (!label.shouldRender) { key *= renderPenaltyMultiplier; }   // default 8

Lower key = attempted earlier. A label that was not drawn last frame gets its key multiplied by 8, pushing it far down the order. In other words already-visible labels are re-attempted first, so the arrangement tends to persist between frames instead of being re-derived from scratch. This one line is a large part of why the pipeline is stable at all, and it is the "priority" half of the hysteresis pair whose "geometry" half is the occlusion tolerance of chapter 24.

Sanity check the direction

Because keys are ascending-priority (small = first), every monotone transformation above must be increasing. Multiplying a positive key by 8 makes it worse; taking log1p of a positive score preserves order; squaring a positive distance preserves order. Any one of these accidentally inverted produces a map that labels the horizon and ignores the foreground — a bug that is instantly visible on screen and completely invisible in a unit test that only checks "the array is sorted".

What a comparison sort actually costs

O(n log n) is a growth rate, not a cost. Turn it into operations for the real n and the motivation for everything in the next four chapters appears.

A comparison sort works by asking questions of the form "does a come before b?" Information theory sets the floor: to distinguish one of n! possible orderings you need at least log₂(n!) ≈ n log₂ n − 1.44n yes/no answers. For our frame:

Three-hundred-fifty-thousand comparisons in a 16 ms budget is not fatal on its own. What makes it expensive is what each comparison is:

  • A data-dependent branch. The CPU speculates which way the comparison will go; in a sort, the answer is essentially random, so it mispredicts about half the time. A mispredict costs on the order of 15–20 cycles, and there is nothing useful to do while the pipeline refills.
  • A function call, in JavaScript. array.sort(comparator) invokes your closure per comparison. Even when it inlines, the sort itself is a generic routine over boxed elements.
  • A pointer chase. Sorting an array of label objects means each comparison dereferences two objects that live wherever the allocator put them, so the access pattern is scattered and the cache hit rate is poor.

The last point suggests the first real optimisation, independent of algorithm: never sort the objects — sort an index array, and read the keys from a flat typed array. The shipped implementation does exactly this, keeping Float64Array keys and Int32Array indices, and materialising the ordered Label[] only once at the very end.

The insight the next chapters exploit

Comparison sorts are general: they work for any comparable type, which is why they must pay log₂ n questions per element. But our keys are numbers in a known, bounded range. That extra knowledge is worth an entire complexity class — with it, you can compute where an element belongs instead of discovering it by interrogation.

14

Counting sort, from scratch

The one primitive to understand. Radix sort is nothing but this, applied a few times in a row.

Suppose the keys are small integers — say every key is 0, 1, 2 or 3. Then you do not need to compare anything. You can count.

Three passes over the data, no comparisons anywhere:

  1. Histogram. Walk the input and count how many items have each key value.
  2. Prefix sum. Turn those counts into starting offsets: bucket 0 starts at 0, bucket 1 starts where bucket 0 ended, and so on. After this step, start[d] is the exact output index where the first item with key d belongs.
  3. Scatter. Walk the input again, and write each item to start[itsKey], incrementing that counter as you go.

In code, all three passes together:

// keys are integers in [0, K)
count.fill(0);
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { count[key[i]]++; }              // 1 · histogram
let s = 0;
for (let d = 0; d < K; d++) { const c = count[d]; count[d] = s; s += c; }  // 2 · prefix
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { out[count[key[i]]++] = src[i]; }        // 3 · scatter
Stability — remember this, chapter 15 depends on it

Because the scatter pass walks the input front to back and each bucket's counter only ever moves forward, two items with the same key come out in the same relative order they went in. A sort with that property is stable. Counting sort is stable for free; it is not an extra feature, it is a consequence of the loop direction. Reverse that loop and you break the next chapter entirely.

15

Radix sort = counting sort, once per digit

Counting sort needs a small key range. A 20-bit key has a range of a million. Radix sort resolves that contradiction by sorting the key a few bits at a time.

Write each key in base 2b — that is, cut its bits into d digits of b bits each. Then run a stable counting sort on the least significant digit first, then on the next digit, and so on up to the most significant. After the last pass the array is fully sorted. This is LSD radix sort.

Why does that work? Consider the state after two passes. The second pass grouped everything by the high digit — correct at the top level. Within one high-digit group, the second pass did not reorder anything, because it was stable: it preserved the order it received, which was the order the first pass produced, which was sorted by the low digit. So each group is internally sorted too. The argument extends to any number of digits: each pass fixes one digit and, by stability, preserves the work of every earlier pass.

The shipped implementation, line by line

This is RadixOrdering from packages/Label/src/Collision/Ordering.ts, complete. Twenty bits, two passes of ten:

// ── 1 · find the observed key range ─────────────────────────────────────
let lo = Infinity, hi = -Infinity;
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { const k = keys[i]; if (k < lo) lo = k; if (k > hi) hi = k; }

// ── 2 · quantise float keys → 20-bit integers ───────────────────────────
const scale = 1048575 / ((hi - lo) || 1);          // 2^20 − 1
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { q[i] = ((keys[i] - lo) * scale) | 0; }

// ── 3 · sort an INDEX array, never the labels ───────────────────────────
let src = idxSrc, dst = idxDst;
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { src[i] = i; }

// ── 4 · two stable counting-sort passes, 10 bits each ───────────────────
for (let sh = 0; sh < 20; sh += 10) {
    cnt.fill(0);                                              // 1024 slots
    for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { cnt[(q[src[i]] >> sh) & 1023]++; }   // histogram
    let s = 0;
    for (let b = 0; b < 1024; b++) { const c = cnt[b]; cnt[b] = s; s += c; }  // prefix
    for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {                                    // scatter, stable
        const d = (q[src[i]] >> sh) & 1023;
        dst[cnt[d]++] = src[i];
    }
    const t = src; src = dst; dst = t;                          // ping-pong buffers
}

// ── 5 · materialise the ordered labels, once ────────────────────────────
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) { out[i] = candidates[src[i]]; }

packages/Label/src/Collision/Ordering.ts — RadixOrdering.run()

Six details in there are deliberate, and each is a small lesson:

  • (x >> sh) & 1023 is the digit extraction. Shift the digit you want down to the bottom, then mask off everything above it. 1023 is ten set bits, so this is "give me bits sh … sh+9".
  • The range is measured, not assumed. lo and hi come from this frame's keys, so all 20 bits of precision are spent on the range that actually occurs — instead of on a fixed near/far domain of which the frame might occupy 2 %.
  • | 0 truncates toward zero and yields a machine integer, which is what keeps the shifts and masks in the integer path rather than round-tripping through doubles.
  • The buffers ping-pong. Each pass reads src and writes dst, then they swap. No allocation, no copying between passes.
  • Only indices move. Labels are touched exactly once, in step 5.
  • Everything is preallocated and grown only when n exceeds capacity. A sort that allocates per frame gives the garbage collector a reason to interrupt you, and a GC pause is a dropped frame no matter how fast your sort was.

Why radix is faster — the actual accounting

Not "because O(n·d) beats O(n log n)". Because of what the operations are, and because the histogram fits in L1 cache.

Count the operations for n = 6 000

Each radix pass touches the data three times: read the digit for the histogram, read it again during the scatter, and write one index. Call that 3n per pass. Plus the histogram: K slots to clear and K to prefix-sum, a cost that does not depend on n at all.

This is the question that makes the trade-off click. Passes and digit width trade against each other: passes = ⌈bits / bitsPerPass⌉. Fewer, wider digits mean fewer trips over the data — but the histogram has 2bits per pass slots, and that grows exponentially.

The knee sits where the histogram still lives in L1. A typical L1 data cache is 32–48 KB; 1 024 four-byte counters is 4 KB, so the whole histogram stays resident across the scatter pass, whose access pattern into it is effectively random. The same quantity in two units

Bucket count and bit budget are the same knob: K buckets is log₂(K) bits of key precision, and a one-pass radix sort is a bucket scatter. 1 024 buckets is exactly 10 bits. Once you see that, the "bucket sort versus radix sort" question in chapter 18 dissolves into a single question about precision.

What radix cannot do

  • It needs non-negative integers. Floats have to be quantised (as here) or bit-manipulated; signed keys need the sign handled explicitly. This is why the pipeline quantises rather than sorting raw Float64 bits.
  • It is not in-place. Two index buffers plus a histogram is the price. At 6 000 elements that is 48 KB of Int32Array — negligible, but it is real memory that must be preallocated.
  • It cannot exploit existing order. A nearly-sorted array — which is exactly what consecutive frames produce — costs a comparison sort like Timsort almost nothing, while radix pays full price every frame. That is a genuine argument for a comparison sort, and it is why the measured difference is 39 % rather than the 3× the operation counts alone would suggest.

Bits, ties, and how much precision you actually need

Quantisation is lossy. The interesting question is what exactly you lose, and the answer is not "accuracy" — it is "the tie-break".

The quantisation step maps a float key to an integer in [0, 2bits):

q = ⌊ (key − lo) · (2^bits − 1) / (hi − lo) ⌋

This is monotone: if key₁ < key₂ then q₁ ≤ q₂. Never >. So quantisation can never invert two labels' priorities — it can only tie them. The resolution is one quantum:

quantum = (hi − lo) / 2^bits

With 20 bits, that is about one millionth of the frame's key range. With 10 bits, one thousandth. With 4 bits, a sixteenth of the range — at which point large groups of genuinely different labels collapse into a single key.

What a tie costs

When two labels tie, the greedy pass takes them in whatever order they happen to sit in the candidate array — which is data order, i.e. essentially arbitrary. Two distinct things go wrong:

  • Placement quality drifts, in either direction. An arbitrary tie-break sometimes packs better than the intended priority order, so the quality metric can even go up. That is not a win; it means the map is no longer honouring the importance policy you defined.
  • Churn rises, and this is the real damage. The key range [lo, hi] is recomputed every frame from the observed keys. As the camera moves, that range slides, so the quantisation boundaries slide with it — and two labels that tie in this frame may separate in the next, in either order. The result is labels swapping places for reasons that have nothing to do with the scene.

Measured across the bench's scenes, 16 bits is the floor at which the quantised order reproduces the exact comparison order's decisions; below that, ties start costing placements and adding churn. The shipped configuration uses 20 bits, which buys margin at no extra cost — because 20 bits at 10 bits per pass is the same two passes as 16 bits would be.

Why 20 and not 16

Passes are what cost time, and ⌈20/10⌉ = ⌈16/10⌉ = 2. Two configurations, identical cost, and one of them has 16× the precision margin. Take the free one.

Bucket sort, the asterisk, and the measured result

Three ordering algorithms, one table. The fastest row is not the row that was shipped, and understanding why is the point of this chapter.

Bucket ordering is the one-pass version, and it is what iTowns did historically: scatter every candidate into one of K buckets over a fixed key domain (derived from the camera's near and far planes), then walk the buckets from near to far. Labels within a bucket keep their input order.

const scale = (K - 1) / ((keyMax - keyMin) || 1);
for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    let b = ((keys[i] - keyMin) * scale) | 0;
    if (b < 0) b = 0; else if (b >= K) b = K - 1;
    buckets[b].push(candidates[i]);        // arrays of arrays — the weak point
}

Two differences from radix matter. The domain is fixed rather than observed, so a frame occupying a narrow slice of the near–far range wastes most of its buckets and ties everything inside one of them. And the buckets are arrays of arrays, so scattering means pushing onto K growable arrays instead of writing into one flat typed array — more allocation pressure, worse locality.

Now the trap. The two starred rows are faster still, and one of them reports higher quality — 70.0 % against 68.4 %. If you were optimising the number in the quality column, you would ship one of those. Do not, for three reasons:

  1. They are not computing the requested order. Ten bits over the observed range, or 1 024 buckets over the near–far range, tie labels whose priorities genuinely differ, and the tie is broken by array order. The 1.6-point gain is the arbitrary tie-break happening to pack this scene slightly better — it is not a better policy, it is a coin flip that landed well.
  2. The gain is not portable. Nothing about it generalises to another dataset, another camera path, or another zoom level. A measurement you cannot explain mechanistically is a measurement you cannot ship.
  3. The remaining speed is negligible. 1.127 → 1.075 ms is 4 % of the collision pass on a budget of 16 000 µs. Trading correct priority semantics for 52 µs is a bad trade in any framing.
A general lesson about benchmarks

A configuration that scores better on your metric while violating a semantic you care about is the most dangerous result a bench can produce, because the number looks like permission. This is why the bench prints an asterisk and why the poster carries the footnote "fewer bits or fewer buckets group labels of similar priority under a single key, which are then separated arbitrarily". Metrics measure what they measure; only you know what you meant.

Four ways to remember which pixels are taken. Three of them answer the identical question and differ only in speed; the fourth changes the question, and that turns out to be the interesting one.

One interface, four structures

Two methods and a contract. Get the contract right and every structure becomes swappable — which is what makes the comparison in chapter 23 meaningful.

interface Occupancy {
    resize(screenW, screenH);      // (re)allocate for a viewport
    clear();                       // zero everything, no reallocation

    testRect(x0, y0, x1, y1, prev) : boolean;   // may this footprint be placed?
    markRect(x0, y0, x1, y1)       : void;      // claim it

    testQuad(qx, qy, prev)         : boolean;   // same, for a rotated quad
    markQuad(qx, qy)               : void;
}

Four properties of that interface do real work:

  • Test and mark are separate. Non-negotiable for multi-piece colliders (chapter 10): all pieces must be tested before any is marked, or a label collides with itself.
  • Everything is in full-resolution screen pixels. A structure that works at a coarser internal resolution converts on the way in. Callers never know or care, which means downscale is not a leaky abstraction.
  • prev is the hysteresis channel — "was this label drawn last frame". Only a previously drawn label may be granted the occlusion tolerance of chapter 24. A brand-new label always needs a clear footprint.
  • clear() does not reallocate. It is called every frame; a structure that allocates in clear() hands the garbage collector a per-frame job.
The invariant that makes tolerance exact

The store only ever contains footprints that were accepted, and an accepted footprint does not overlap anything already there. So the stored rectangles are mutually disjoint — which is what lets the grid compute a candidate's covered area by simply summing its pairwise intersections, with no risk of double counting. If you ever add a code path that marks without testing, that invariant dies and the tolerance arithmetic silently becomes wrong.

20

Brute force, uniform grid, R-tree

The three structures that store shapes and compare them exactly. They place identical labels; only their bookkeeping differs.

Brute force — the baseline you must measure

Keep an array of accepted rectangles. For each candidate, test it against all of them. It is O(n²), it is trivially correct, and at 6 000 candidates it measures 42.2 ms per frame — about three frames' entire budget for one stage. Implement it first anyway: it is the reference every other structure is validated against, and any structure that disagrees with it (outside of deliberate raster approximation) has a bug.

Uniform grid — what MapLibre, Mapbox and iTowns use

Cut the screen into fixed cells (32 px in the shipped default). Each accepted rectangle is registered in every cell it touches. A query visits only the cells the candidate touches and tests only the rectangles found there.

// insert: register in every cell the rect touches
for (gy = y0/cell … y1/cell)
  for (gx = x0/cell … x1/cell)
    grid[gy·gw + gx].push(id);

// query: visit those cells, test each occupant once
stamp++;
for (each cell) for (each id in cell) {
    if (seen[id] === stamp) continue;      // already tested via another cell
    seen[id] = stamp;
    if (aabbOverlap(candidate, rect[id])) return false;
}

Two implementation notes carry most of the performance:

  • The stamp trick. A rectangle spanning four cells would otherwise be tested four times. A monotonically increasing stamp per query, written into a seen[] array, dedupes in O(1) with no clearing between queries — you only ever compare against the current stamp value.
  • Cell size is a two-sided trade. Cells much smaller than a label mean each label registers in many cells (insertion cost, memory); cells much larger mean each query scans many irrelevant occupants. The rule of thumb — and what the bench's sweep confirms — is a cell close to the typical label's larger dimension.

Measured: 2.883 ms, a 14.6× speedup over brute force, with bit-for-bit identical placement.

R-tree — what OpenLayers uses

A balanced tree of bounding boxes: leaves are the stored rectangles, each internal node holds the bounding box of its children, and a query descends only into nodes whose box intersects the query. Asymptotically it is the better structure — O(log n) query, no dependence on a cell size, and it adapts to non-uniform density, which is why it dominates in GIS work over unbounded coordinate spaces.

Here it measures 4.137 ms — 44 % slower than the flat grid. Three reasons, all specific to this workload rather than criticisms of the structure:

  • The domain is bounded and small: 1920 × 1080 pixels. A grid over a bounded domain needs no tree to find the right region — an integer divide does it.
  • The tree is rebuilt every frame, because the whole occupancy is rebuilt every frame. Insertions with node splits are more expensive than pushing onto an array.
  • Traversal is pointer chasing. A grid query is arithmetic plus a linear scan of a small array; a tree query is a chain of dependent loads, which the prefetcher cannot help with.
Measured, and worth stating plainly

Brute force, grid and R-tree all score 77.7 % quality and 96.3 churn — identical to three significant figures. They are three implementations of one predicate, so this is the expected result, and seeing it is the best available evidence that all three are implemented correctly. Any claim that one spatial index "labels better" than another is a claim that one of them is buggy.

21

The occupancy bitmap

Stop storing shapes. Store the screen — one bit per cell — and the question changes from "which shapes do I intersect" to "are these bits clear".

The idea comes from chart labelling (Kittivorawong et al., IEEE VIS 2021) and it inverts the data structure: instead of a list of occupied rectangles, keep a raster of the screen where a set bit means "taken". A candidate is accepted when its footprint's bits are all clear. There is no n in that sentence — cost depends on the label's own size, not on how many labels are already placed.

Bits, packed into words

A 1920 × 1080 bitmap is 2.07 million cells. As bytes that would be 2 MB to clear every frame; as bits it is 259 KB; and packed into 32-bit words it is 64 800 words, which you can clear with one fill(0) and test 32 cells at a time. Rows are padded to whole words:

wordsPerRow = (W + 31) >> 5;                  // ceil(W / 32)
bits = new Uint32Array(wordsPerRow * H);
// cell (x, y) lives in word (y · wordsPerRow + (x >> 5)), at bit (x & 31)

x >> 5 is "divide by 32" and x & 31 is "remainder mod 32" — the two operations you need constantly, both single instructions because 32 is a power of two.

The span test, and its two masks

Testing a horizontal run of cells from x₀ to x₁ means testing part of the first word, all of the words in between, and part of the last. The partial words are handled with masks:

maskA = 0xffffffff << (x0 & 31);              // keep bits from x0 upward
maskB = 0xffffffff >>> (31 - (x1 & 31));      // keep bits up to x1

The test walks rows and exits at the first evidence of occupancy — the common case for a busy screen, so most rejections cost one read:

function regionEmpty(bm, wpr, x0, y0, x1, y1) {
    const wA = x0 >> 5, wB = x1 >> 5;
    const maskA = (0xffffffff << (x0 & 31)) >>> 0;
    const maskB = (0xffffffff >>> (31 - (x1 & 31)));
    for (let y = y0; y <= y1; y++) {
        const row = y * wpr;
        if (wA === wB) {
            if ((bm[row + wA] & maskA & maskB) !== 0) return false;
        } else {
            if ((bm[row + wA] & maskA) !== 0) return false;
            for (let w = wA + 1; w < wB; w++) if (bm[row + w] !== 0) return false;
            if ((bm[row + wB] & maskB) !== 0) return false;
        }
    }
    return true;
}

Downscale: the knob no other structure has

Nothing forces one bit per screen pixel. Use one bit per 2×2, 4×4 or 8×8 block and both the memory and the number of word reads fall quadratically. The conversion has to round outward so a footprint is never understated:

shift = log2(downscale);   m = (1 << shift) - 1;
a = x0 >> shift;                            // floor for the low edge
b = min(W - 1, (x1 + m) >> shift);          // ceil for the high edge

That rounding is what makes the bitmap conservative: a label's footprint is dilated by up to one cell on each side, so it can be rejected for touching a cell it does not really touch, but it can never be accepted while genuinely overlapping. Conservative in the safe direction — the direction where you lose a label rather than draw two on top of each other.

That single choice explains the quality column in chapter 23: at full resolution the bitmap scores 71.2 % where the exact structures score 77.7 %, and at a quarter resolution it drops to 64.3 %. You are buying speed with fidelity, explicitly, on a dial. No object-based structure offers that dial at all — and the bench measures the penalty at −3 to −4 % placement at downscale 1 versus −23 to −24 % at downscale 8.

Counting instead of asking: occlusion tolerance

Because the bitmap stores cells rather than shapes, it can answer a quantitative question — "what fraction of this footprint is already occupied?" — which no object index can answer cheaply. Counting set bits in a word is a single instruction on real hardware and a five-line trick in JavaScript:

function popc(v) {                          // count set bits in a 32-bit word
    v = v - ((v >>> 1) & 0x55555555);          // pairs
    v = (v & 0x33333333) + ((v >>> 2) & 0x33333333);   // nibbles
    return (((v + (v >>> 4)) & 0x0f0f0f0f) * 0x01010101) >>> 24;   // bytes → sum
}

And the tolerance test never counts more than it must, exiting as soon as the answer is decided in either direction:

occupied > limit                       → deny   (already too covered)
occupied + cellsStillToCome ≤ limit    → accept (cannot possibly exceed)

Same predicate as counting the whole footprint, a fraction of the reads. What the tolerance means — and why it only ever applies to labels that were already drawn — is chapter 24.

Hierarchies over bits

If a bitmap scan is linear in the footprint, a coarse summary should reject or accept large regions in one read. Two ways to build one; one of them earns its keep.

The word summary — branching 128, and it works

Level 0 is the flat bitmap. Level 1 has one bit per word × 4 rows of level 0 — one bit summarising 32 × 4 = 128 cells — and level 2 does the same again over level 1. A summary bit is set when anything below it is set, so:

  • a clear coarse bit proves the whole region below is empty — accept without touching level 0;
  • a set coarse bit proves nothing except "look finer", so exactness always comes from level 0.

One packed word compare at level 2 can clear a 1024 × 16 cell region. Because the fine level is still a bitmap, count-based tolerance keeps working.

The quadtree pyramid — and why it disappoints

The textbook alternative halves the resolution at every level and descends from the coarsest, recursing only into set cells. It is elegant, it is what a graphics programmer reaches for, and in this workload it underperforms the flat bitmap and the word summary alike. The reasons are instructive:

  • Branching factor 4 versus 128. A quadtree level removes two bits of address per level; the word summary removes seven. Reaching a useful coverage takes many quadtree levels, each an extra dependent memory access.
  • The flat scan is already fast. Its access pattern is sequential words within a row — the best case for the prefetcher. A hierarchy replaces cheap sequential reads with fewer, scattered, dependent ones, which is not automatically a win.
  • Coarse levels stop helping when the screen fills. Early in a frame, when everything is empty, the top level accepts immediately. Late in a frame — which is where the time goes, because that is when most candidates are being rejected — nearly every coarse cell is set, so every query descends anyway and the coarse levels are pure overhead.
  • Levels silently no-op. Once a level collapses to 1 × 1 it can never reject anything. A fixed level count therefore does nothing at low resolutions or large downscales, which is the kind of parameter that looks tuned and is not.
The general shape of this result

Hierarchies pay when the failure they avoid is expensive and common. Here the common case is a rejection, and a rejection on a flat bitmap already costs one word read thanks to the early exit. There was never much for a hierarchy to save. This is worth remembering before adding a tree to anything: measure what the common case costs first, because you can only optimise what is actually being paid.

The exact structures are interchangeable

Identical quality, identical churn, 14.6× spread in time. Choosing among them is purely an engineering decision, and on a bounded screen with per-frame rebuilds the flat grid wins it.

The bitmap trades quality for stability, and the trade is the reason to take it

Full-resolution bitmap gives up 6.5 points of quality against the grid — the conservative dilation of chapter 21 — and in exchange churn falls from 96.3 to 77.1, a 20 % reduction in flicker. It is also slower than the grid in this table, which is worth stating plainly rather than glossing: the bitmap was not chosen for speed at equal fidelity.

Why would rasterising reduce flicker? Because a quantised decision boundary is a stable decision boundary. With exact geometry, two labels a hair apart flip their verdict as soon as sub-pixel camera motion nudges them across the touching threshold — and that motion happens every frame. On a raster, the verdict only changes when the footprint crosses a whole cell boundary, so small camera movements change nothing at all. The visual difference in a dense scene between the exact grid and the quarter-resolution bitmap remains minor, which is what makes the dial usable rather than theoretical.

Bitmap at full resolution, occlusion tolerance 0.1. Not the fastest and not the highest quality — the most stable, and the only one that can be dialled down on a weak device without changing a line of code. With the shipped collider and radix ordering, this configuration lands at 0.983 ms.

Part VI

Time

Everything so far decided a single frame. A map is a sequence of frames, and the sequence has requirements of its own that no single-frame metric can see.

24

Flicker, hysteresis, and the tolerance

Two mechanisms, one in the ordering and one in the geometry, both driven by a single bit: was this label drawn last frame?

Run the pipeline of chapter 3 with no memory of the previous frame and it behaves correctly and looks awful. Labels appear and vanish continuously, because a sub-pixel camera movement is enough to reverse a marginal decision — and in a dense scene, a large fraction of decisions are marginal. The fix is hysteresis: make the current state slightly self-preserving.

Mechanism 1 — the priority boost

From chapter 12: a label not drawn last frame has its key multiplied by renderPenaltyMultiplier (8). Already-drawn labels are therefore attempted before newcomers and generally keep their space. This costs nothing — one multiply per candidate — and it operates entirely in the ordering stage.

Mechanism 2 — the occlusion tolerance

The geometric half, and the one whose semantics are most often misread:

A new label

Needs a completely empty footprint. Strict, binary, early-exit. The tolerance does not apply.

An already-drawn label

Is kept while it is occluded by at most tol of its area — 10 % at the shipped setting.

Three consequences follow directly from that asymmetry, and each of them protects you from a wrong conclusion:

  • Tolerance cannot improve quality on a cold frame. With nothing drawn yet, every label is new, so the strict path is the only path. If your quality metric is measured on a cold frame, tolerance will appear to do nothing — correctly.
  • It cannot create a permanent overlap. The overlap it permits is bounded by tol and only ever arises transiently, while a label that was legitimately placed is being encroached upon.
  • Its effect shows up in churn. That is the column to watch when tuning it, and the correlation to expect is negative against churn and positive against dwell time (the mean number of consecutive frames a label stays visible).

What it costs, measured

From Tab 1: the quarter-resolution bitmap with tolerance 0.1 reaches churn 72.8 at 64.3 % quality, against 77.1 and 71.2 % for the full-resolution bitmap. From Tab 4, the shipped configuration at half resolution reaches churn 63.5 at 64.7 % quality against 68.5 and 68.4 % at full resolution. The pattern is consistent: every step towards stability costs placement, in the low single digits of percent per step. There is no setting that gives both, and the right operating point depends on whether your users are panning or reading.

One more mechanism, not yet shipped

A third form of stability is worth knowing about: active ranges (Been, Daiches, Yap, 2006). Instead of deciding per frame, precompute for each label the interval of zoom levels over which it is displayed, and guarantee that a label appears and disappears exactly once across a continuous zoom. That eliminates flicker by construction rather than by damping — at the cost of a global precomputation that assumes a zoom-like camera, which a free 3D camera does not provide.

Part VII

Practice

How to measure this without fooling yourself, how the result compares to the reference implementation everyone knows, and the order to build it in.

25

Benching it honestly

Every number in this course came out of a bench. Benches lie in specific, repeatable ways, and these are the ones that matter here.

Build the ceiling, then attack it

The annealed optimum makes every other number meaningful, so it has to be trustworthy. Two checks:

  • Prove what you can. On sparse parts of the scene, MWIS is solvable exactly (isolated labels and small components can be brute-forced). Report the fraction of candidate weight that was proven versus annealed — the bench reports "6 % proven exactly, the rest annealed", which tells you the ceiling is a lower bound on the true optimum, and therefore that your quality percentages are, if anything, slightly flattering.
  • Check that it converges. Run the annealer twice as long. If the ceiling moves by more than a fraction of a point, it had not converged and every percentage below it was against a moving target.

Score everything against one ground truth

A configuration using per-glyph boxes and one using whole-label boxes must be scored with the same notion of overlap, or you are comparing each against its own private yardstick — which lets a coarse collider score above 100 %. The bench scores every configuration against the labels' true inked footprints (per-glyph oriented quads). Because every collider model contains its label's ink, any placement valid under any model is also ink-disjoint, so the single ink-based ceiling is a valid upper bound for all of them.

Know how big a difference is real

Before believing a 0.3-point quality difference, re-run with a different scene seed and a different camera path. Differences that survive both are structural; differences that do not are the scene. As a working rule from these runs, treat sub-half-point quality gaps and sub-5 % time gaps as noise unless you can explain the mechanism — and note that JIT warm-up alone can produce a 5 % time difference between the first and third run of the identical configuration.

Invariants worth asserting in a test

  1. No two drawn labels overlap under the ground-truth collider. This is the specification; everything else is an optimisation.
  2. Placed ≤ candidates, and quality ≤ 100 %. Either violation means the scoring and the placement disagree about geometry.
  3. Determinism. The same frame in must produce the same decisions out, twice in a row. Non-determinism here usually means a structure was not fully cleared.
  4. Radix equals comparison. Property-test the ordering against Array.sort on random keys — with duplicates, which is where a stability bug shows.
  5. test() does not mutate. Calling it twice must give the same answer.
  6. All pieces tested before any is marked. Assert it in the multi-piece path; this bug is invisible except as uniformly poor placement.

Build it in ten steps

In this order, each step verifiable before the next. The order matters: it keeps you always able to answer "is it still correct?"

  1. Project one label. World position → screen AABB (chapter 5). Draw the box over the label and confirm it tracks under rotation and zoom. Nothing else works until this does, and every later bug will look like a collision bug.
  2. Brute force, no ordering. Store accepted boxes in an array, test each candidate against all of them (chapter 20). Slow and correct. This is your reference implementation forever.
  3. Add the priority key and a real sort. log1p(distSq · sizeFactor), then Array.sort (chapter 12). Confirm the visual policy is what you want — big and near wins — before optimising the sort.
  4. Add the metric harness. Placed count, weighted quality, churn between consecutive frames, milliseconds. Without this, every later step is a guess. This is the step people skip, and skipping it is why label systems get tuned by opinion.
  5. Swap in a uniform grid. Cell size ≈ typical label width, with the stamp trick (chapter 20). Assert the placement is bit-identical to brute force — if it is not, the grid is wrong, and this is the easiest bug in the whole system to find at this stage.
  6. Subdivide the line-label collider. Per-glyph boxes, testing all pieces before marking any (chapter 10). Expect the largest single quality jump of the project here — up to 15 points, per Tab 2 — and expect to hit the self-collision bug once.
  7. Add hysteresis. The prev flag through test(), and the priority boost for already-drawn labels (chapter 24). Watch churn fall; watch quality dip slightly. Both are correct.
  8. Replace the sort with radix. Quantise to 20 bits over the observed range, two passes of 10 (chapter 15). Property-test the output against Array.sort on random keys including duplicates, then measure.
  9. Add the bitmap occupancy. Packed Uint32Array, regionEmpty / regionSet, a downscale shift, then popcount tolerance (chapter 21). Its placement will not match brute force — it is conservative by construction — so check the direction of the difference: strictly fewer labels, never an overlap.
  10. Build the ceiling and the sweep. Anneal an offline optimum, then sweep the axes as a product (chapter 25). Only now can you tune parameters on evidence, and only now will the numbers you produce mean anything to anyone else.
If you only do three of them

Steps 4, 5 and 6. The metric harness, the grid, and the per-glyph line collider give you most of the quality and most of the speed of the finished system; everything else in this course is refinement measured in single points.

What is still open

Four directions, in the order their expected value suggests.

  • Off-screen pre-filtering. The candidate phase still visits every registered label to decide it is off-screen. A spatial index in world space — or reuse of the renderer's own frustum culling — would make the phase proportional to what is visible rather than to what exists. With 24 000 labels resident and a few thousand visible, this is the largest remaining structural win in the pipeline.
  • Early exit when placement becomes impossible. Late in a dense frame almost every remaining candidate is rejected, and each rejection still costs a projection plus a test. A saturation signal — the screen is full at this scale, stop trying — would cut the tail. The bench's dead-cell experiments are a first attempt: track consecutive failures per coarse region and skip candidates whose every region is saturated.
  • Decoupling the collision rate from the frame rate. Nothing requires a full collision pass every frame. A stationary camera needs none at all; a fast-moving one can tolerate a stale answer for a frame or two, because the labels are moving faster than the eye can read them anyway. The config already carries stationaryThreshold and updateRate for this.
  • Automatic placement. Everything here assumes one fixed anchor per label. Allowing K candidate positions per label (the classic eight-position model, or MapLibre's variable anchors) is the single largest available quality gain — and it is a genuinely harder problem, because the greedy pass now chooses where as well as whether. The bench has a PAL mode to measure it.
The four sentences worth keeping
  • Subdivide curved labels. One box per label is the single worst decision available; per-glyph boxes recover fifteen points.
  • Do not pay for oriented tests at glyph granularity. They buy 3 % of quality for twice the time.
  • Sort by counting, not comparing. 20 bits in two passes of 10 reproduces the exact order 39 % faster; the histogram must fit in L1.
  • Measure churn. A configuration that wins on quality and loses on churn loses.
